Absolutely. For your first research meeting with your PhD advisor, I recommend preparing reusable expressions for five situations: opening the meeting, summarizing what you've read, explaining your understanding, asking questions, and discussing next steps.

One important tip: you don't need sophisticated English to sound like a good researcher. Clear, simple sentences that communicate your reasoning are much more valuable than complicated sentences that are hard to produce spontaneously.

I'll focus on expressions that sound natural in an American university research meeting, especially when discussing systems, the Linux kernel, and research papers.

## 1. Starting the meeting

You don't need a long introduction. A few natural sentences are enough.

GREETING

“Hi Professor Liu! How are you doing?”

안녕하세요, 교수님! 잘 지내셨어요?

TRANSITION TO RESEARCH

“I've been looking into the Linux kernel scheduler and reading the paper you recommended.”

Linux 커널 스케줄러를 살펴보고 추천해 주신 논문을 읽고 있었습니다.

INTRODUCING YOUR SUMMARY

“I'd like to briefly share my understanding and get your feedback.”

제가 이해한 내용을 간단히 공유하고 피드백을 받고 싶습니다.

IF THE PROFESSOR ASKS HOW THINGS ARE GOING

“It's going well! I've been getting familiar with the codebase and trying to understand the main ideas.”

잘 진행되고 있습니다! 코드베이스에 익숙해지면서 핵심 아이디어를 이해하려고 하고 있습니다.

## 2. Explaining what you understand

These are probably the most important expressions to practice. You don't need to present everything as a definitive conclusion. Researchers often explain their current understanding and invite correction.

“My understanding is that …”

제가 이해하기로는 …

Example: “My understanding is that the main issue is the interaction between the upper-level scheduler and the kernel scheduler.”

“The way I understand it is that …”

제가 이해한 방식으로는 …

Example: “The way I understand it is that the kernel scheduler doesn't have full visibility into the application's scheduling decisions.”

“What I found interesting is that …”

제가 흥미롭게 느낀 점은 …입니다.

Example: “What I found interesting is that the behavior at the application level can affect kernel-level scheduling.”

“One thing I'm not entirely sure about is …”

제가 아직 확실히 이해하지 못한 부분은 …입니다.

Example: “One thing I'm not entirely sure about is how the scheduler handles this situation.”

“I might be missing something, but …”

제가 뭔가 놓치고 있을 수도 있지만 …

Example: “I might be missing something, but wouldn't this approach introduce additional overhead?”

My recommendation: Get comfortable with `My understanding is that ...` and `One thing I'm not entirely sure about is ...`. They let you explain your thinking without pretending to know everything.

## 3. Asking your advisor questions

The goal is to ask questions that show you're thinking about the research, even when you're uncertain.

“Could you elaborate on that a little?”

그 부분을 조금 더 설명해 주실 수 있을까요?

Use this when you understand the general idea but want more detail.

“Could you help me understand why …?”

왜 …인지 이해하는 데 도움을 주실 수 있을까요?

Example: “Could you help me understand why this approach doesn't work at the kernel level?”

“How should I think about this problem?”

이 문제를 어떤 관점에서 바라보면 좋을까요?

Excellent for research meetings. You're asking for a way to frame the problem, not just a factual answer.

“Are there any papers you'd recommend I read next?”

다음으로 읽으면 좋을 논문이 있을까요?

“Would it make sense to explore this direction further?”

이 방향을 좀 더 탐구해 보는 게 좋을까요?

## 4. When you don't understand what your advisor says

This is especially useful because spontaneous academic speech can be difficult to follow, even if you know all the technical vocabulary.

“Sorry, could you say that one more time?”

죄송하지만 한 번만 더 말씀해 주실 수 있을까요?

“Could you clarify what you mean by …?”

…가 무슨 의미인지 조금 더 명확히 설명해 주실 수 있을까요?

“Let me make sure I understand. Are you saying that …?”

제가 제대로 이해했는지 확인하고 싶은데요. …라는 말씀이신가요?

Example: “Let me make sure I understand. Are you saying that we should focus on the interaction between the two schedulers first?”

“I see. So the key issue is …, right?”

알겠습니다. 그러니까 핵심 문제는 …라는 거죠?

Useful for confirming your understanding and showing active listening.

“I haven't thought about it from that perspective.”

그런 관점에서는 생각해 보지 못했습니다.

A natural response when your advisor introduces a new angle.

You don't need to apologize every time you miss something. Asking someone to repeat or clarify a point is normal in research discussions.

## 5. Discussing research directions and next steps

Your first meeting is also an opportunity to understand what your advisor expects you to explore.

“What do you think would be a good starting point?”

어디서부터 시작하면 좋을까요?

“What would you like me to focus on first?”

제가 우선적으로 어떤 부분에 집중하면 좋을까요?

“I was thinking of starting with …”

우선 …부터 시작해 볼까 생각하고 있었습니다.

Example: “I was thinking of starting with the existing implementation and tracing how the scheduler makes its decisions.”

“I'll look into that and see what I can find.”

그 부분을 살펴보고 무엇을 알아낼 수 있는지 확인해 보겠습니다.

“I'll read the paper and try to come up with some ideas.”

논문을 읽고 몇 가지 아이디어를 생각해 보겠습니다.

“Would it be helpful if I put together a short summary for our next meeting?”

다음 미팅을 위해 간단한 요약을 정리해 오면 도움이 될까요?

## 6. Ending the meeting naturally

You don't need a formal closing speech.

* “Thanks, this was really helpful.” — 감사합니다. 정말 도움이 됐습니다.

* “I'll follow up on these points before our next meeting.” — 다음 미팅 전에 이 부분들을 추가로 살펴보겠습니다.

* “I'll work on that and keep you updated.” — 그 부분을 진행하면서 계속 공유드리겠습니다.

* “Thanks for your time. I'll see you next time!” — 시간 내주셔서 감사합니다. 다음에 뵙겠습니다!

A particularly useful expression is “I'll follow up on that.” It means you'll take further action on something you discussed.

## 7. A realistic mini-conversation to practice

Imagine your advisor asks what you've been working on.

Advisor

“So, what have you been working on?”

You

“I've been reading the paper and getting familiar with the Linux kernel scheduler. I'd like to briefly share my understanding and get your feedback.”

Advisor

“Interesting. What do you think is the main challenge?”

You

“My understanding is that the challenge lies in the interaction between the application-level scheduling and the kernel scheduler. But I'm not entirely sure how to approach that yet.”

Advisor

“You might want to look at how the two layers communicate.”

You

“I see. So I should first investigate how the two layers interact, right? Are there any particular papers you'd recommend?”

Advisor

“Yes, I'll send you a couple of papers.”

You

“That would be great. Thanks, this was really helpful. I'll read them and follow up on these points before our next meeting.”

## 8. Practice the expressions interactively

Let's practice the most important skill: explaining your understanding without needing to prepare every sentence in advance.

Practice 1

Your advisor asks:

“What's your current understanding of the problem?”

Try answering in English in 2–3 sentences. You can talk about a research paper, the Linux kernel scheduler, or any topic you're currently studying.

My final advice: prioritize these three sentence starters before your first meeting.

1. “My understanding is that …”

2. “One thing I'm not entirely sure about is …”

3. “What do you think would be a good starting point?”

If you can use those naturally, you'll have a foundation for explaining ideas, acknowledging uncertainty, and asking your advisor for guidance. You can build the rest of your research-meeting English around them.
