#### For journaling, lean on __phrasal verbs__ (end up, stick around, get to, creep in), __hedge words__ (actually, maybe, kind of), and __comparative phrases__ instead of "from the perspective of." Those three habits alone shift writing a long way toward natural.

#### Word choice: Latin/French roots over Anglo-Saxon

English has two vocabularies layered on top of each other. Everyday words (mostly from Old English) feel warm and natural; scholarly synonyms (mostly borrowed from Latin and French centuries ago) feel formal. Academic writing systematically picks the second set.

"skipping overtime" → "refrain from working overtime"
"staying late" → "remain at the office"
"gets to me" → "exert a subconscious influence"
"going home" → "departing"
"a thought keeps creeping in" → "this observation prompts a question"
"what should I do" → "what would constitute an appropriate response"

Notice how refrain, remain, exert, depart, constitute, prompt all feel "elevated" — that's because they came into English through medieval scholarship, not daily life.

#### Nominalization: turning verbs into nouns

Academic writing converts actions into abstract nouns, which makes sentences denser and more impersonal.

"I don't work overtime" → "my decision to refrain from working overtime" (the action becomes "a decision")
"I noticed this" → "this observation" (noticing becomes "an observation")
"other ways to see it" → "alternative interpretations" (ways of seeing become "interpretations")

The writer disappears behind these noun phrases — you're left examining concepts rather than watching someone act.

#### Impersonal framing

Version 2 starts with "Does skipping overtime actually protect..." — you, doing something. Version 3 starts with "It is worth examining whether..." — no agent, just a topic floating in space. This "it is worth ~ing" / "it is necessary to ~" pattern is one of the most reliable signals of academic register. The writer treats themselves as a case study, not a person.

Same move at the end: "what should I do about it" (you, deciding) → "what would constitute an appropriate response on my part" (the response exists as an abstract object you're identifying).
