# What is difference between Latinate verbs and Anglo-Saxon style?

This is one of the most useful things to understand about English, because the whole register system (casual vs. formal) is built on it.

**Why English has two vocabularies**

Old English (spoken before 1066) was a Germanic language — close cousin to German, Dutch, Old Norse. Then the Normans conquered England in 1066 and ruled in French for about 300 years. During that time, French (which descends from Latin) became the language of the court, law, religion, and learning, while Anglo-Saxon English stayed the language of farmers, workers, and daily life.

When the two finally merged into modern English, the language kept *both* sets of words. So English often has two (or three) words for the same idea: one from the kitchen, one from the courtroom.

**The classic example — animals**

The farmers who raised the animals spoke Anglo-Saxon. The nobles who ate them spoke French. So:
- *cow* (A-S) → *beef* (French *bœuf*)
- *pig* (A-S) → *pork* (French *porc*)
- *sheep* (A-S) → *mutton* (French *mouton*)
- *deer* (A-S) → *venison* (French *venaison*)

The living animal kept its peasant name; the meat got an aristocratic one.

**General characteristics**

| | Anglo-Saxon | Latinate |
|---|---|---|
| Length | short (1–2 syllables) | long (3+ syllables) |
| Feel | concrete, physical, emotional | abstract, conceptual, neutral |
| Register | speech, intimate writing | formal, academic, legal |
| Endings | varied, often hard consonants | -tion, -ity, -ate, -ize, -ment |
| Impact | hits harder | feels more distant |

**Pairs you'll recognize**

- *ask* → *inquire* → *interrogate*
- *begin* → *commence* → *initiate*
- *end* → *finish* → *terminate*
- *help* → *aid* → *assist*
- *buy* → *purchase*
- *get* → *obtain* → *acquire*
- *show* → *demonstrate*
- *think* → *consider* → *contemplate*
- *use* → *utilize*
- *need* → *require*
- *want* → *desire*
- *house* → *residence* → *domicile*
- *big* → *large* → *substantial*
- *talk* → *converse* → *communicate*

Each step right = more formal, more distant, more abstract.

**Phrasal verbs are almost all Anglo-Saxon**

This connects to what I said about your version 2. Phrasal verbs (a verb + small preposition) are the most Anglo-Saxon thing in English, and academic writing systematically replaces them with single Latinate verbs:

- *give up* → *surrender / relinquish*
- *look into* → *investigate*
- *put off* → *postpone / defer*
- *find out* → *discover / ascertain*
- *bring about* → *cause / generate*
- *get rid of* → *eliminate*
- *come up with* → *devise / propose*
- *think about* → *consider*
- *talk about* → *discuss*

If you want your writing to sound academic, *the single fastest change* is hunting down phrasal verbs and swapping them for single Latinate verbs. If you want it to sound natural and warm, do the opposite.

**How to feel the difference**

Read these two sentences out loud:

> *"She came in and sat down."*
> *"She entered and was seated."*

Same event, totally different worlds. The first one *happens* in front of you. The second one is *reported* to you. That's the Anglo-Saxon vs. Latinate split in one breath.

**A practical takeaway**

Native writers don't pick "100% Anglo-Saxon" or "100% Latinate" — they mix, and the mix sets the tone. A diary leans ~80% Anglo-Saxon. A casual email is maybe 70/30. A business email tilts to 50/50. An academic paper flips to 30/70 or further. Once you can hear which bucket a word falls into, you can dial your writing's register up and down on purpose — which is exactly what you were doing across your three versions.


### Casual Writing

#### For journaling, lean on __phrasal verbs__ (end up, stick around, get to, creep in), __hedge words__ (actually, maybe, kind of), and __comparative phrases__ instead of "from the perspective of." Those three habits alone shift writing a long way toward natural.

### The underlying logic for formal writing

All these moves serve one goal: distance. Academic writing positions the writer as a neutral analyst, even when the subject is themselves. Version 2 says "here is what is happening in my head right now." Version 3 says "here is a phenomenon worth analyzing, which happens to involve me." Same content, opposite stance toward the reader.

So if you ever want to push your own writing in the academic direction, the quickest levers are: swap phrasal verbs (get to, end up, stick around) for single Latinate verbs (affect, result in, remain), turn your actions into noun phrases, and start sentences with "It is..." or "This..." instead of "I...".

#### Word choice: Latin/French roots over Anglo-Saxon

English has two vocabularies layered on top of each other. Everyday words (mostly from Old English) feel warm and natural; scholarly synonyms (mostly borrowed from Latin and French centuries ago) feel formal. Academic writing systematically picks the second set.

"skipping overtime" → "refrain from working overtime"
"staying late" → "remain at the office"
"gets to me" → "exert a subconscious influence"
"going home" → "departing"
"a thought keeps creeping in" → "this observation prompts a question"
"what should I do" → "what would constitute an appropriate response"

Notice how refrain, remain, exert, depart, constitute, prompt all feel "elevated" — that's because they came into English through medieval scholarship, not daily life.

#### Nominalization: turning verbs into nouns

Academic writing converts actions into abstract nouns, which makes sentences denser and more impersonal.

"I don't work overtime" → "my decision to refrain from working overtime" (the action becomes "a decision")
"I noticed this" → "this observation" (noticing becomes "an observation")
"other ways to see it" → "alternative interpretations" (ways of seeing become "interpretations")

The writer disappears behind these noun phrases — you're left examining concepts rather than watching someone act.

#### Impersonal framing

Version 2 starts with "Does skipping overtime actually protect..." — you, doing something. Version 3 starts with "It is worth examining whether..." — no agent, just a topic floating in space. This "it is worth ~ing" / "it is necessary to ~" pattern is one of the most reliable signals of academic register. The writer treats themselves as a case study, not a person.

Same move at the end: "what should I do about it" (you, deciding) → "what would constitute an appropriate response on my part" (the response exists as an abstract object you're identifying).
